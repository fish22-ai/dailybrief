Global bond market steadies after sharp sell-off
FT Markets · 2026-10-02 13:09
查看英文摘要Show English excerpt
Heavy selling this week pushed 10-year US Treasury yields to their highest level since 2002
发生了什么What happened
Global bond markets suffered a sharp sell-off this week, with the 10-year U.S. Treasury yield rising to its highest level since 2002.
市场为什么在意Why it matters
Markets had expected interest rates to peak and bond markets to stabilize, but yields breaking through multiyear highs mean bond investors are demanding greater compensation, and all assets anchored to the risk-free rate (equities, credit bonds, and real estate) need to be repriced.
金融传导Transmission
①Global bond sell-off→10Y U.S. Treasury yield↑ to its highest level since 2002→Bond prices fall→Risk-free rate Rf rises→Equity discount rates rise→Present values of highly valued assets shrink
②At the same time, higher yields attract cross-border capital inflows into U.S. dollar assets→U.S. dollar strengthens→Emerging-market currencies and debt come under pressure
展开解析Show analysis收起解析Hide analysis7 条7 notes
  • What is the 10-year U.S. Treasury yield? It is the yield to maturity (YTM) on 10-year Treasury notes issued by the U.S. Treasury, regarded globally as a benchmark for the risk-free rate Rf. It is the annualized return investors earn by buying the bonds and holding them to maturity.
  • Why does the yield matter? It is the anchor for global asset pricing. Equity valuations, corporate bond financing costs, and mortgage rates all reference it. When the risk-free rate rises, discount rates for all risky assets rise along with it, making future cash flows worth less when discounted to the present.
  • Bond prices and yields move inversely: When bonds are sold heavily in the market, bond prices fall, allowing new buyers to buy at lower prices, so the yield to maturity rises for the same coupon.
  • Why is it the highest since 2002? This means current interest rates have returned to levels seen more than twenty years ago, requiring a fundamental adjustment in valuation logic for investors accustomed to a low-interest-rate environment.
  • What is the term premium? It is the additional compensation investors require for holding long-term bonds rather than rolling over short-term bonds, because the longer the lock-in period, the greater the uncertainty. When selling intensifies, the term premium widens and long-end yields rise faster.
  • Why would the U.S. dollar strengthen? Higher U.S. Treasury yields mean higher returns on U.S. dollar assets, drawing global capital into the United States in pursuit of yield and boosting demand for the dollar.
  • Why do emerging markets come under pressure? A stronger U.S. dollar means higher repayment costs for emerging markets' dollar-denominated debt, while capital outflow pressure intensifies and local currencies depreciate.

展开解析Show analysis收起解析Hide analysis7 条7 notes
  • What is yield curve control (YCC)? The Bank of Japan commits to keeping the 10-year government bond yield at an extremely low level (such as around 0%) through unlimited bond purchases, aiming to lower borrowing costs across the economy.
  • What is a carry trade? Investors borrow yen at extremely low interest rates, convert it into higher-interest-rate currencies (such as the U.S. dollar or Australian dollar), and invest in high-yielding assets to earn the interest rate differential. These trades are enormous in scale and have been an important driver of global asset gains over many years.
  • Why are carry trades vulnerable to Japanese rate hikes? If yen interest rates rise, the cost of borrowing yen increases, narrowing or even eliminating the scope for carry profits. Investors then need to unwind their positions, selling overseas assets and converting the proceeds back into yen to repay their debt, triggering a global asset sell-off.
  • What is Abenomics? It is the combination of ultra-loose monetary policy, fiscal stimulus, and structural reforms pursued by former Prime Minister Shinzo Abe, centered on using yen depreciation and monetary easing to combat deflation and stimulate growth.
  • Why is deflation frightening? Prices keep falling, consumers delay spending because they expect prices to be lower in the future, and companies suffer declining revenue and lay off workers, creating a vicious cycle. Japan was trapped in deflation for more than twenty years.
  • What does the Takaichi government's different policy direction mean? It means no longer relying solely on monetary easing to stimulate demand, instead emphasizing improvements in supply capacity and fiscal discipline. Markets need to reassess Japanese government bond supply and demand and the interest rate path.
  • How does yen appreciation affect Japan's stock market? Yen appreciation reduces Japanese exporters' overseas profits when translated back into yen, often putting pressure on the Nikkei index; however, lower import costs benefit consumers.

  • Why would a U.S. diesel export ban push up European diesel prices? Europe is one of the main destinations for U.S. diesel exports. If the United States stops selling, Europe will need to find other suppliers, but global diesel supply is inelastic in the short term, so prices rise when demand exceeds supply.
  • Why does Trump want to do this? Ahead of the November midterm elections, U.S. domestic gasoline and diesel prices are among voters' top concerns. Lowering oil prices helps electoral prospects.

  • Why would U.S. domestic oil prices fall as a result? Lower exports mean more diesel stays in the domestic market, increasing supply and lowering prices. But this comes at the expense of exporters' interests.
  • How might the market trade this news? Go long European diesel futures (expecting prices to rise), short U.S. diesel futures (expecting prices to fall), or go long upstream energy stocks that benefit from higher oil prices.
  • What are the risks of this type of policy? An export ban could violate WTO rules and provoke retaliation from trading partners; U.S. refiners might also cut production, reducing domestic supply instead and making the policy counterproductive.
